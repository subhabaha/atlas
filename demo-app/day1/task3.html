Failure Taxonomy & Hypothesis Backlog Console

Where four qualified engineers disagree on the category, no model can learn a boundary that is not there. The disagreement is not noise to be cleaned — it is the deliverable, and it sets a hard ceiling on everything you build downstream.

The scenario

The installed-base service organisation closes thousands of work orders a year, each carrying a few words of free text written by an engineer standing next to a machine. Somewhere in that text is the ranked list of what is actually going wrong — and nobody has ever counted it, because counting it requires agreeing what the categories are.

Your team builds the console that does the counting, and discovers early that four qualified engineers do not agree on the categories either. That discovery is the deliverable.

Confidence is not agreement — the trap in this task

The model returns clean, confident, well-formatted categories for all 200 records — including the 7 the humans could not agree on. It says "unclear" on ~1% of records where the humans said it on 12%. Look specifically where humans split: that is where the model's answer is least trustworthy and most likely to be accepted without challenge, because it arrives without hesitation. An annotator that never says "unclear" on genuinely unclear text is not a good annotator — it is a fast one.

Count, then weight by cost — two Pareto charts, different top two
Frequency alone misleads; frequency × impact is the ranking that survives a budget conversation. The most frequent category is rarely the most expensive one, and a backlog built from raw counts sends engineering effort to the wrong place.

Worked example — the highest-priority row

Rewriting the definitions for the two most-confused categories (seal/leak vs lubrication/oil) is estimated to raise inter-annotator agreement from κ 0.52 to 0.70 — from "moderate" to "substantial", usable ground truth — in three days. No model change touches the ceiling; this does. That is why it outscores everything below it.

FIX THE TAXONOMY BEFORE THE MODEL — the boundary does not exist yet
At κ 0.52 the labels cap any classifier near 0.52 consistency. Work bearing/rotating first (€4.08M/yr headroom) though seal/leak is the most common. Spend the first three days rewriting the two most-confused definitions to lift κ to 0.70 — a data-and-definition move, not a modelling one.

The traps this console exposes

Chasing accuracy above the agreement ceiling

A model cannot be more consistent than the labels it is trained on. At κ 0.52, a 90% target asks the classifier to resolve boundaries four qualified humans could not. Effort spent on the model there is spent below the ceiling; the ceiling itself moves only when the definitions do.

Ranking a backlog by raw count

Seal/leak is the most frequent category and bearing is the most expensive. A backlog built from counts sends engineers to seal/leak and leaves €4M of bearing failures untouched. Frequency × impact is the only ranking that survives contact with a budget.

Costing the "unclear" bucket

A large unclear bucket is a defect in your taxonomy. Fold it into a euro figure and you hide exactly the thing you need to see. Report it as a count, always — never as a cost.

The lesson this build makes undeniable

A classifier's realistic ceiling is the agreement of the people who labelled it.

Measure inter-annotator agreement before you measure a model. If four engineers agree at κ 0.52, that is the ceiling — and the cheapest, highest-value work is not a bigger model but rewriting the two definitions that cause most of the disagreement. Data and definitions move the ceiling; models only approach it.

Defend your result

"Our four engineers agree at κ 0.52. Ranked by count the top problem is seal/leak; ranked by cost it is bearing/rotating. We would work on bearing/rotating first, worth roughly € 4.08M a year, and we would not chase seal/leak despite it being the most common. First, though, we rewrite the two most-confused definitions to lift agreement from 0.52 to 0.70 — because no model beats its labels."
