Failure-Prediction Operating-Point Studio

A model can be 99% accurate and prevent nothing. Class imbalance, target leakage and a finite number of engineers turn "accuracy" into a decision the business cannot use — until you re-express it as an operating point in alerts per engineer per day, with an interval on every number.

The scenario

Atlas Copco has roughly 1,200 installed machines under a service contract, each scored once an hour by a failure-prediction service. Six field service engineers cover the region. Your team has been handed a model that reports very high accuracy and asked one question: can we switch it on?

By the end of this lab you have an answer, a number, an interval on that number, and an operating point defended in alerts per engineer per day — not a leaderboard accuracy.

What the attendees must build

  • A bootstrap 95% interval on recall everywhere recall appears (~100 positives → noisy)

Why the leaking model is a mirror, not a model
The single most valuable screen you build today

Ask at what moment each value becomes known. The five mode columns are written after the machine failed, in the service report. "Parts issued from stores", "work-order closed", "downtime hours" — every one is a consequence of the failure sitting in the database looking exactly like a feature. The leaking model scores 0.99 on everything and detects nothing you did not already know.

Feature representation beats model choice
Recall gain (at 0.5) from three physics-derived features — mechanical power (τ·2π·rpm/60), temperature difference (proc − air), overstrain product (wear·τ) — versus the gain from swapping logistic regression for a gradient-boosted tree. Three lines of arithmetic from process knowledge win, and usually by a wide margin.

"Get more data" and "try a bigger model" are the two most expensive wrong answers in industrial AI. The power-failure mode is defined by power leaving a band; hand the model power and it stops guessing. This is the data-centric argument produced by your own hands, not asserted from a slide.

The alarm budget — the constraint that decides everything
First correct the prior: the benchmark is 3.4% positive, but a real installed base develops roughly two dispatch-worthy degradations per day across 1,200 machines — π = 0.17%, twenty times rarer. This is label shift. The model's ranking transfers; its probabilities and every threshold on them do not.

Corrected precision: TPR·π / (TPR·π + FPR·(1−π)), with π = 0.0017, not 0.034. Quoting the training prior overstates precision by more than an order of magnitude.

Put this on screen in large type
14 of every 15 alerts an engineer opens will be nothing.

Even a perfect model — catching every degradation, at the maximum volume this organisation can absorb — hits precision ≈ 6.7%. That is not a modelling failure. It is arithmetic, forced by a rare event and a finite number of engineers. No amount of accuracy escapes it.

At the budget operating point

Holding FPR at the 2.3% the organisation can absorb, the honest model detects about 48% of degradations (95% CI 38–58%). The cost-optimal threshold sits lower and would demand far more than five alerts per engineer — so the honest deliverable is a triage step that dismisses an alert from the desk for € 35 instead of dispatching an engineer for € 700, not a lower threshold.

The label and the mode columns do not agree, and the RNF count does not match the documentation. RNF is a random failure generated at a fixed probability, unrelated to any feature — more data will not help, a bigger model will not help. The correct engineering response is to quantify it, exclude it from the improvement target, and stop spending money on it.

The traps this studio exposes

Accuracy on an imbalanced label

96.6% accuracy is what you get for predicting "no failure" forever. Any accuracy figure quoted on a 3.4%-positive label without a confusion matrix beside it is a number engineered to impress a room that has not read the base rate.

The prior you trained on is not the prior you deploy into

The training set is 3.4% positive; the installed base is 0.17%. Quote precision at the training prior and you overstate it twenty-fold. Recompute precision at π = 0.0017 before you say a single number to the business — the model's ranking transfers, its probabilities do not.

Recall without an interval does not place

The test split holds ~100 failures. Recall computed on 100 positives is noisy; a point estimate hides that. Bootstrap the test set 2,000 times and carry the 95% interval everywhere recall appears — including on the slider above, which recomputes it live.

The lesson this build makes undeniable

The deliverable is an operating point, not an accuracy.

A model's value is decided by a rare event, a finite number of engineers, and the cost of each error — not by a leaderboard. The honest answer is a sentence in the units the business acts on: alerts per engineer per day, recall with an interval, precision at the deployment prior, and a triage step that dismisses for €35 rather than dispatches for €700.

Defend your result

"At five alerts per engineer per day we catch ~48% of degradations, 95% CI 38% to 58%, and 1 in 15 alerts is a real one. The leaking model claimed 99% — from columns that do not exist at prediction time. The largest honest gain came from three derived physics features (+0.19 recall), not the model. We recommend switching it on as a ranked worklist behind a €35 desk-triage step, because at this fleet size and prior no threshold makes it an auto-dispatcher."
