Production Readiness Review

You made exactly one change overnight and called it System B. Now do the thing almost no proof of concept does: prove, properly, whether B is better — paired test, power, calibration, drift — and produce a review a release board could sign.

The scenario

System A is the Task 2 model — a gradient-boosted tree on the raw AI4I sensor columns. Overnight you made one change: you added the three derived physics features. That is System B. The question is not "is B nice" — it is "is B better, and can we prove it to a board?"

This task delivers all seven items on the Day 1 assignment slide, ending in a single verdict and the strongest argument against that verdict.

The comparison — paired, because item difficulty must cancel
An unpaired two-sample test on the same evaluation set is the most common statistical error in AI work. Items differ in difficulty; unpaired comparison treats that variation as noise, and the noise is usually larger than the improvement. Pair, and item difficulty cancels. Power — could this gold set have detected it at all?
The familiar "n per group" table is for two independent groups — the wrong design. Part 1 told you to pair. Under McNemar the power depends only on the discordant pairs: n_discordant ≈ (zα/2+zβ)² / (pb−pc)² × (pb+pc).

The finding worth more than the comparison it invalidates

Our evaluation set produced only 24 discordant pairs. It can confirm a large, obvious win and nothing subtler — our gold set is an order of magnitude too small to detect a 5-to-10-point improvement. Halving the effect roughly quadruples the sample required.

The peeking simulator — why "check until significant" is not a test
Two identical systems. Evaluate after every few accumulated items and stop the moment p < 0.05. The nominal 5% false-positive rate climbs steeply — with frequent enough checking it passes 30%.
The fix, in one line

Fix the sample size and the stopping rule in advance, or use a sequential design that accounts for planned interim looks (alpha spending). The more often you peek at an undecided experiment, the more certain you are to declare a winner that does not exist.

Ship B behind the €35 desk-triage operating point, with the PSI > 0.25 retraining trigger armed on day one. Strongest counter-argument against my own verdict: the interval's lower bound is only +0.02 on just 24 discordant pairs, and two top-five features already breach the PSI trigger — the measured gain may not survive the covariate drift that is present today, and a gold set an order of magnitude larger could shrink it toward zero.

The traps this review exposes

Unpaired testing on the same evaluation set

Comparing two systems with an independent two-sample test throws away the fact that they saw identical items. Item difficulty becomes noise, and that noise is usually larger than the effect. Pair the outcomes and difficulty cancels — McNemar looks only at the items where the systems disagree.

Peeking until significant

"Run it until p < 0.05" is not a test; it is a way to manufacture a winner from two identical systems. The false-positive rate climbs past 30% with frequent checking. Fix n and the stopping rule before you start, or spend alpha on planned looks.

A review that only argues for itself

A readiness report signed by people who had no way to know what was omitted is advocacy, not evidence. Requiring the strongest counter-argument in the same document is the cheapest defence against the failure mode that ends most proofs of concept. If the facts cannot produce a real counter-argument, you did not measure enough.

The lesson this build makes undeniable

Proving a change helped is a design, committed before the result.

Pre-register the metric and the pass criterion. Pair the comparison so difficulty cancels. Check whether your sample could ever detect the effect. Never peek. Calibrate and watch for drift. Then state one verdict — and the strongest argument against it — because a well-defended DO NOT SHIP outscores a poorly-evidenced SHIP.

Defend your result

"System B raises recall from 0.60 to 0.72, absolute +0.118, paired 95% CI [+0.02, +0.21], permutation p 0.024 — one extra failure caught per 250 machines scored. It meets our pre-registered +0.10 criterion, so we SHIP WITH LIMITS. What would have made us decide the other way: a lower bound at zero, which is one order-of-magnitude-larger gold set away, and the two features already past PSI 0.25."
