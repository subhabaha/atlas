Why Does the Model Fail?

Between 10% and 30% of your "model failures" are labelling failures — and you cannot know which without looking at the failures one by one. This bench diagnoses before it proposes, and the finding that changes the conversation is almost never the one people expect.

The scenario

A trained surface-crack inspection model comes back with a headline score that is not good enough, and everyone in the room already has an opinion about why — more data, a bigger backbone, more augmentation. The lab exists to make you diagnose before you propose: extract features once, then iterate on the classifier, the labels and the capture conditions in seconds.

The corpus is 40,000 real concrete-surface photographs (cracked / uncracked). We seal two corruptions into it at rates only we know — label noise and capture-condition shift — so that later the audit can be checked against the truth it was hiding.

What the attendees must build

  • Overall accuracy never shown without per-class recall beside it — the automatic-deduction rule made structural
  • The 50 highest-confidence false negatives + 50 false positives, rendered as a grid you actually open and tag
  • A five-category failure taxonomy: small · low-contrast · occluded · specific-class · benign-shadow, with counts
  • A label audit on 30 reviewed failures by two independent reviewers — how many were mislabels, not model errors
  • Cohen's kappa between the two reviewers = the model's realistic ceiling, and the headline recomputed with corrections
  • Recall broken down by capture-condition group — one group carries a disproportionate share
  • A ranked taxonomy and one sentence naming the constraint, with the evidence that rules out the other three
  • Licence position recorded for both dataset and pretrained weights, with the date checked

The one sentence

The constraint is labels. It is not more data (row 1 & 6: more data adds inconsistency and has no effect on borderline-part disagreement); it is not capture as the primary limit (low-light hurts, but corrected recall still trails the label ceiling); it is not model capacity (κ 0.78 caps consistency at ≈ 89%, and 30% of failures were mislabels a larger model would have learned to reproduce). The honest fix is a written guideline with boundary examples and a re-label of the affected subset.

The trap Copilot will hand you

One number, no per-class breakdown

Ask for "accuracy" and you get 90.1% and a green tick. That number is worth minus ten marks today, because it hides that the defect class recalls at 0.847 while the easy class sits at 0.955 — the entire failure is in the class whose miss escapes to the customer. Toggle "Apply audit-corrected labels" above and watch the honest headline move without a single training run.

"Collect more data" before ruling out labels and capture

The default proposal is a collection programme. But four of the six constraint rows are unmoved by more data, and 30% of the reviewed failures were mislabels — every one of those is a case where more data makes things worse. Recommending collection without the evidence that rules out labels and capture first is the other minus-ten habit this lab exists to break.

The augmentation that lies about your process

Copilot will offer horizontal flips and 90° rotations because every tutorial has them. On a fixed camera over a fixed fixture, each is a false claim about your process — you would be teaching the model to ignore an orientation that carries information. Every augmentation is a claim; check it with someone who knows the line before applying it to a million images.

The lesson this build makes undeniable

Label consistency sets the ceiling — your model cannot be more consistent than the ground truth it learns from.

A headline score is a starting question, not an answer. The diagnosis that changes the conversation with management is not "the model is 90%" — it is "30% of what we called model failures were labelling failures, our reviewers agree at κ 0.78, and a 95% target is asking the model to be more consistent than the labels it was trained on." That finding is invisible until someone opens the failure grid and looks.

Defend your result

"Our headline was 90.1%. Per class it was 0.847 on cracked and 0.955 on uncracked. Of 30 audited failures, 9 were labelling errors — we injected 11%, and the audit found label errors at 3× that rate because it looked where the model fails. Our two reviewers agreed at κ 0.78. Recall on the low-light group was 15 points below the rest. The constraint is labels, and it is not more data because four of six situations are unmoved by it and a third of our failures never were model errors."
