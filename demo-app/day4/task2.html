Error Analysis Report & Three Hypotheses

The cost-optimal threshold needs no new data, no labels and no training run — and on many industrial systems it is the largest single available gain, and the one teams check last. But it is only available if the review volume it creates fits inside real capacity.

The scenario

Produce the report you would hand your engineering manager, and the three hypotheses you would ask them to fund. Then — because this is a build, not a document — run one of the three and report whether it held, including if it under-delivered.

The report leads with the Task 1 label audit, because that is the line that turns an argument about model quality into a finding about process quality — and it comes with a number.

The review-volume arithmetic — do it before choosing the threshold, not after
Any threshold that defers uncertain cases to a person creates a workload. On a line at two parts per second the constraint is far tighter than for documents.

Why it fails on day one

Review must fit inside the 0.5-second cycle time or the part must be diverted to an offline station. At the cost-optimal operating point the deferral creates 8,640 reviews/shift — 3.6× the two offline stations' capacity of 2,400. A review design that exceeds capacity fails immediately, and the threshold that produces it is not available to you, whatever the cost curve says. Either raise the threshold (accept more escapes) or add offline review capacity — and put both on the same page as the cost saving.

The trap this report exists to close

Choosing the threshold before doing the arithmetic

The cost curve says 0.45 and saves €78k/shift, so teams ship it — and discover on day one that it defers 8,640 parts to two review stations that can clear 2,400. The saving was never real, because the operating point was never available. Do the review arithmetic first; the threshold you can actually run is the one whose deferral fits.

An unpaired test on paired data

Both models saw the same images, each right or wrong per image. That is McNemar's exact case. Comparing two accuracy figures with a two-sample proportion test throws away the pairing and is the most common statistical error in vision work — it will call a real effect noise, or noise a real effect.

The report where everything worked

H2 is statistically significant and still misses its bar. Reporting only the win — "re-labelling helped, p<0.01" — hides that most label noise sits away from the boundary and a full re-label would under-return. A report in which every proposed change succeeded is a report nobody experienced believes.

The lesson this build makes undeniable

A saving on a cost curve is a proposal, not a decision, until the arithmetic and the capacity agree.

The cost-optimal threshold is free — no data, no labels, no training run — and it is often the biggest single gain on the table. But "cost-optimal" and "available" are two different tests. The operating point you ship is the cheapest one whose review volume fits real capacity, whose per-slice effects harm no critical class, and whose hypotheses were pre-registered so a miss is a finding rather than an excuse.

Defend your result

"The cost-optimal threshold saves € 78k per shift against 0.5, with no retraining, and per-class operating points add a further € 54k. We pre-registered the re-labelling hypothesis and it under-delivered — significant at p=0.001 but +0.041/+0.052, below our +0.06 bar. At that operating point we defer 12%, which is 8,640 reviews per shift against a capacity of 2,400, so the threshold is not available until we add review stations or accept more escapes."
