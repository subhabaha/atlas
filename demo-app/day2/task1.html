Pattern Selection Bench

With four labelled failures you cannot train a supervised model — and, more to the point, you cannot evaluate one. Anomaly detection asks a question the data can answer. The confidence interval on recall is not a footnote; its width is the architecture decision.

The scenario

Five industrial use cases are on the table; each has a tension in it. The team produces a defensible pattern decision for all five, then settles Case 2 empirically — the case where engineering instinct and the evidence point in opposite directions.

Case 2 as written: vibration-based failure prediction on 300 compressors, with eleven labelled failures in three years. Almost every team's first answer is a supervised classifier. Today you build one and discover that the problem is not that it performs badly — it is that you cannot tell whether it performs badly.

The group-leakage gap, in one sentence

Consecutive readings from the same compressor seconds apart are almost identical, so a random row split puts near-duplicates on both sides — the test set is effectively part of the training set. The model memorised four episodes and is congratulated for recognising them. Switch to the event split and the AUC collapses to a coin toss on the hardest fold.

The measurement that actually settles the case
Forget performance. With four positive events, how precisely could recall be measured even if the model were perfect? Bootstrap the CI over the events.

Catching three of four events gives recall 0.75 — but the interval spans from 0.30 to 0.95. You cannot distinguish a good model from a poor one, cannot show an improvement, cannot fail a model that deserves to fail. An architecture you cannot evaluate is one you can never improve.

The trap the confidence hides

An excellent, unmeasurable score

Write the row-split scores on the dashboard and they will be applauded. But four failure episodes cannot be split honestly — leave-one-out gives four folds, and one of them is a coin toss. The answer to Case 2 is not "supervised learning performs worse". It is "supervised learning cannot be evaluated here."

Fitting that touches the failure dates

If any normalisation, scaler or threshold is fit on data that includes April–August, the detector has seen the answer. Fit on the documented Feb–March normal window only, then score everything after. Anything else leaks the very events you are trying to detect ahead of time.

A score without a signal name

An anomaly detector that emits a number gives a maintenance engineer something to ignore. One that names the deviating signal — TP2 and reservoir pressure moved before motor current — gives them an air leak to go and find. Explainability was one of the twelve selection criteria; this is what it looks like in practice.

The lesson this build makes undeniable

Ground-truth availability chooses the pattern before performance ever enters the room.

Anomaly detection is not the fallback for when supervised learning is weak. It is the correct pattern when your labels are too scarce to measure a supervised model at all. The confidence interval on recall is the whole argument: when it runs from 0.30 to 0.95, the question the case was really asking was never "which model?" — it was "which pattern can this data support?"

Defend your result

“For Case 2 we chose anomaly detection because with four labelled events the interval on recall runs from 0.30 to 0.95 — we could never have measured a supervised model. Our detector gives a mean 27 hours of lead time at 4.2 alerts per engineer per day across 300 compressors, on a threshold we can tune. The biggest risk is a slow-onset leak inside the reservoir band, which is why we run two detectors and report the one miss.”
